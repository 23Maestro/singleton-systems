# Freelance pricing

Price the project. Keep time as an internal safety check.

## Start with buyer context

When the buyer has not named a budget, ask:

> What budget range are you trying to stay within for this?

Then resolve only the missing facts that can move the quote:

- Final deliverable and runtime
- Raw footage, clip guide, selects, and asset prep
- Reference style and motion load
- Deadline and true rush window
- Revision rounds
- Platform fee and desired net payout
- Expected repeat volume

Treat the buyer's budget as a fit signal. It is not an automatic ceiling.

## Build the quote

Judge three layers:

1. Buyer value: urgency, intended use, repeat work, and what the finished piece
   lets the buyer ship.
2. Production risk: unclear footage, culling, research, sourcing, story work,
   motion, revisions, and unfamiliar workflows.
3. Transaction cost: marketplace fees, rush compression, paid assets, payout
   delay, and direct-client overhead.

Use $30 per expected work hour as Jerami's current private floor unless he gives
a different floor for the job. Do not present that math as the offer. Do not
infer a standing hourly rate from one project quote. Do not lead the client
reply with an hour estimate unless the client asks for it.

For a marketplace quote, calculate the gross price from the desired net:

```text
gross quote = desired net / (1 - confirmed fee rate)
```

Confirm the fee from the current contract or Jerami's stated number. Do not
assume one platform fee applies to every contract.

Choose the cleanest commercial shape:

- Accept the named budget when the defined scope fits and the private floor
  clears.
- Counter with a smaller scope when the budget is real but the work does not fit.
- Give a range when the buyer has not supplied enough source material.
- Use a paid test when both sides need proof before recurring work.
- Use hourly billing only while the scope remains unknowable.

Do not cut a price without naming what changed: prep, runtime, polish, sourcing,
turnaround, or revisions.

When automation reduces Jerami's labor after the quote, keep the gain. Reprice
only when the client changes the scope or the original quote used a false scope
assumption.

## Read client signals

Silence after a quote is a real negative signal. Treat price as a likely factor
when the timing supports that inference. Do not call it proven without a client
statement. Record the confidence and any competing explanation.

Give a new quote time to breathe. Do not lower it in response to ordinary reply
lag. Follow up once after a reasonable interval. When the client names a lower
competing bid or a cash limit, mark price as a confirmed fit factor and choose
between narrower scope, the original price, or a clean decline.
